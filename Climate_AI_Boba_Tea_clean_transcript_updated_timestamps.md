### Why methane matters

**[1:50] Yishan:** Olya, let’s talk about methane. Give us the basic version of what you’re working on.

**[2:15] Olya:** Methane is responsible for about 30% of the warming we’re experiencing today. There are far fewer methane molecules in the atmosphere than CO₂ molecules, but each one has a much stronger warming effect.

Our mission is to capture methane at its source and neutralize it. We build devices that go on livestock-manure ponds, coal mines, and similar sources. We’ve also worked in permafrost. Once installed, the machines continuously capture and destroy methane and measure the result very precisely. There can also be benefits involving fertilizer, water quality, and air quality.

**[3:12] Yishan:** Thirty percent of current warming is enormous. Most people have barely heard about methane as a climate issue. Why is something that large so underrepresented?

**[3:42] Olya:** That’s a good question, and the imbalance extends to finance. Something like 2% of climate finance goes to methane, and almost all of that goes to detection. I’ve found that mysterious for more than a decade. Methane isn’t a newly discovered problem.

There has been progress. Landfills produce substantial methane, for example, and US landfills have gradually become regulated. More than a hundred countries have also joined the Global Methane Pledge. But in general, the problem remains extremely under-hyped.

### Choosing a neglected problem

**[4:47] Olya:** That neglect is exactly why I entered the field. My rough heuristic for where I can make an impact is:

> Size of the problem ÷ number of people working on it

Assuming the problem is solvable, the higher that ratio is, the more likely my effort will make a difference.

**Yishan:** So you chose methane because it offered unusually high leverage for a skilled engineer and founder?

**Olya:** Exactly. I’m a software engineer by training. Initially I went into the electric grid, which is a natural entry point for software engineers interested in climate. You look at the grid and see auctions and optimization problems—it looks like classic computer science.

But everybody and their grandmother was working on the grid. Even if I did an extraordinary job, perhaps I could help reduce global emissions by a few percent. I wondered whether there was a problem larger than that with fewer people working on it.

I found a database with roughly a hundred lines showing where emissions came from and went through it line by line. I reached methane and thought, “What is this? Why is so little being done about it?” Then I went down the rabbit hole and never really came back out.

### Burrito drones and methane cameras

**[6:21] Yishan:** Was that before you started Frost?

**Olya:** Yes. I was at Google working on the electric grid when I got bitten by the methane bug.

**Yishan:** That sounds dangerous.

I said, “Great. Replace the burrito with a methane camera. Fly a lawn-mower pattern over oil-and-gas fields and find all the leaks.”

### Why detection does not automatically cause repair

**[8:13] Yishan:** Engineers tend to assume that if we can detect exactly where something bad is happening, everyone will be eager to fix it.

**[8:27] Olya:** Unfortunately, there can be a perverse incentive. If you know a leak exists, you may have to report or repair it. If you don’t know, that obligation may not exist.

At some point the operator compares the cost of losing a little product with the cost of frequent inspections and repairs. If the policy environment doesn’t penalize ignorance, it can be cheaper to remain ignorant.

Some companies run very tight operations because they don’t want to lose product. Others operate more sloppily because fixing everything costs more than the lost gas. Under the current policy landscape, both kinds of companies can remain in business.

**[9:56] Yishan:** So the efficient operators capture more of the product they can sell, while the sloppy operators simply accept the loss.

**Olya:** Exactly. The data shows both cases. The best oil-and-gas companies may lose around 0.2% of production at their wells, while the worst can approach 10%. In the Permian Basin, the upstream average is roughly 2.4% or 2.5%.

### Frost focuses on mitigation

**[11:02] Yishan:** What does Frost actually do? Are you detecting leaks, repairing them, or something else?

**Olya:** We’re strictly a mitigation company. There are already many good detection technologies. Detection matters for oil and gas because there are more than a million miles of pipeline: almost all of it works, and you need to locate the small percentage that doesn’t.

We work on sources whose locations are already known. Dairy and pig farms are permitted operations. We know where they are and approximately how many animals they have. With that information and weather data, you can make a good estimate of how much methane a particular manure pond produces. The issue isn’t finding the source. The source is fixed.

**[12:19] Olya:** The challenge is creating an incentive to mitigate it. We connect methane destruction to carbon markets and divide the resulting value between Frost and the landowner. We handle the entire chain: capture, destruction, measurement, carbon-market connection, project financing, installation, and operation.

### The combustion engineering

**[12:40] Yishan:** When you say “destroy” the methane, do you mean you burn it? How does that work?

**[13:08] Olya:** We burn it. The difficult part is that the incoming gas isn’t pure or consistent. It might be 40% methane one day and 75% the next. It can contain around a thousand parts per million of hydrogen sulfide, which is extremely corrosive. Flow rates are low in winter and high in summer.

We need complete combustion across all those conditions. The system also has to detect problems in upstream farm equipment—leaks in the pond cover, problems with the manure pump, and other anomalies.

There’s a sensor and anomaly-detection problem, then a tight controls problem: safely destroying methane across a wide range of concentrations and flow rates. For sources of this size, I don’t know of another service that is fully remote-controlled, continuously monitored, connected to carbon markets, and essentially “set it and forget it.”

### Why burning methane is better than releasing it

**[14:10] Yishan:** You passed over something that everyone will wonder about: why is it environmentally good to burn methane? Burning things is normally considered bad.

**Olya:** Methane in the atmosphere eventually turns into CO₂ anyway. The difference is that before it does, it traps a tremendous amount of heat.

Methane also contributes to smog. The associated air pollution causes premature deaths and crop losses. Destroying the methane promptly prevents much of that damage.

You do have to control the combustion carefully. People dislike flaring partly because it can produce nitrogen oxides. If the temperature remains below roughly 1,500 degrees Celsius, however, NOx production is essentially negligible.

We’ve calculated the PM2.5 and smog effects. Depending on the pollutant, controlled combustion is around 98% to 99% better than doing nothing.

**[15:27] Olya:** Manure-pond gas also contains ammonia, a major precursor of PM2.5 in the developed world. Our system prevents that ammonia from escaping, which is why the PM2.5 benefit is so large.

### Methane’s short-term potency

**[16:08] Yishan:** How long does methane remain in the atmosphere, and how much more heat does it trap than CO₂?

**Olya:** Methane’s atmospheric half-life is roughly nine to twelve years before it converts into CO₂. Call it ten years to make the math easy.

**[16:38] Olya:** Over a 20-year period, methane traps about 84 times as much heat per unit as CO₂.

**Yishan:** Let me make sure I understand. Methane leaks from sloppy oil-and-gas operations, manure ponds, and rice production. For roughly 20 years, it has about 84 times the warming effect. Your equipment converts it into CO₂ immediately. CO₂ is normally the greenhouse gas everyone wants to avoid, but here it replaces something 84 times more potent.

**Olya:** Exactly. Relative to many climate interventions, it’s remarkable leverage.

**[18:00] Yishan:** So controlled fire is the way to fight climate change. I should clarify that I’m not advocating ecoterrorism.

**Sunny:** Did you just say fire is the way to fight climate change?

**Yishan:** Yes. We figured it out. This business is amazing.

**Olya:** Fire is the right approach for at least some methane sources. High-temperature destruction is also used for certain refrigerants.

### Exploding hills in Siberia

**[30:25] Yishan:** Tell us the origin story.

**Olya:** I was reading the *Siberian Times*. It published surprisingly good science coverage, along with UFO stories.

**[31:04] Olya:** It reported exploding hills of methane in Siberian permafrost.

**Yishan:** Did you say “exploding hills of methane”?

**Olya:** Correct. On the Yamal and Gydan peninsulas in Russia, hills began appearing in the permafrost. One exploded loudly enough to be heard. Investigators found a charged piece of permafrost. Satellite images showed that something had formed a hill and then blown the top off it, leaving a lake or crater.

Even after the site opened to the atmosphere, methane concentrations remained very high, suggesting an active release.

Professor Katey Walter Anthony at the University of Alaska Fairbanks had documented concentrated methane releases in Alaskan permafrost too. Thankfully, we didn’t go looking for hills that were about to explode. We examined the craters and lakes left afterward, where methane can continue bubbling out.

### Scientific uncertainty in the Arctic

**[32:43] Olya:** We wanted to know how many of these concentrated releases existed, how much methane came from each one, whether they were a rounding error or a major source, and whether climate change was making them worse.

Those are exceptionally hard questions. Permafrost covers roughly 9% of Earth’s surface and is difficult to observe. Hyperspectral satellites don’t work especially well over ice and snow for this purpose.

Professor Walter Anthony agreed these were legitimately difficult scientific questions. We asked whether we could join an expedition and build a device that would measure and mitigate one of the sources.

### The first device

**[34:35] Olya:** A few friends and I built the first device as a side project over a summer. We took it into the field and gathered some measurements. We had hoped for continuous data, but the equipment failed after a while. Arctic field work is hard.

We then asked whether other methane sources had the same basic characteristics but were easier to access and service. The answer was yes—many of them. A significant share of atmospheric methane comes from sources that nobody is required to address.

I don’t blame farmers. A farm is essentially operating its own wastewater-treatment system while also feeding cattle, maintaining barns, growing crops, and running a business. If society wants farmers to do additional environmental work, society should pay for it.

### Farm revenue and responsibilities

**[35:42] Yishan:** How much can a farmer make from one of your projects?

**[35:57] Olya:** It depends on herd size, methane production, and the farm’s manure-spreading schedule. We have farmers making around $100,000 per year with us.

**Yishan:** One hundred thousand dollars a year is substantial—potentially a new fancy truck every year.

**Olya:** For some farms it may be more. It can be a meaningful source of revenue, especially because we try to leave them with no project expenses.

**[36:26] Yishan:** You finance it, automate it, monitor it, and provide on-site service. What does the farmer actually have to do?

**[36:44] Olya:** The main recurring task is pumping rainwater off the manure-pond cover.

We put an impermeable cover over the pond. Rainwater collects on top instead of mixing with the manure. That’s helpful—the farm no longer has to haul that extra water—but the water must occasionally be pumped away so its weight doesn’t damage the cover.

If there is a problem with the farm’s own manure pump or related equipment, the farmer repairs that, as they normally would. The rainwater is still fresh water, so it can go into a freshwater pond or be used productively.

### Why manure ponds exist

**[38:13] Yishan:** A manure pond is exactly what it sounds like?

**Olya:** Yes: a great deal of cow manure in a large, shallow pond.

Large farms are required to store manure so it doesn’t wash into rivers. When it’s time to plant crops, the farm spreads the manure on fields as fertilizer. It contains nitrogen and phosphorus, so keeping it in a pond supports nutrient cycling.

The problem is that manure may remain in storage for six months, producing methane the whole time. On average, the farm also loses roughly half the nitrogen.

**[39:23] Olya:** For a thousand-cow dairy, that can mean losing around 39 tonnes of nitrogen. The farm then has to buy synthetic fertilizer to replace it—perhaps around $23,000 at current fertilizer prices.

Covering the pond keeps most of that nitrogen in the manure. The nutrients go onto the fields, into crops, into the cows, and back into the pond. Livestock farming can therefore be more circular than municipal wastewater treatment.

Cities import food and send nutrients into wastewater systems. Large wastewater plants process tremendous volumes in a small footprint, but the recovered nutrients often do not return to food-producing fields; they may go to golf courses, road medians, or other uses.

### The Manure Expo

**[41:18] Yishan:** I did not expect this Space to involve quite so much discussion of literal pools of manure.

**Olya:** Then you should know there is a Manure Expo every year.

**Yishan:** A what?

**[41:41] Olya:** The Manure Expo. It showcases equipment for every part of handling livestock manure.

**Yishan:** You’ve attended?

**Olya:** Yes. It was great. I highly recommend it.

**Yishan:** What was great about it?

**Olya:** A farm has many decisions to make, with increasing levels of technology. The expo I attended near Cornell demonstrated the entire chain: moving manure from the barn to the pond through pipes or trucks; mixing and managing it in the pond; and applying it to fields.

You can spread manure on top of crops or cut into the soil and inject it underneath, reducing runoff and helping more nitrogen reach the plants. The expo showed everything from half-million-dollar machines that automate the process to Amish horse-and-buggy systems.

Most of us are separated from the infrastructure behind food production. We picture a cow being fed or milked, but not the extensive systems around it. The expo made those hidden systems visible.

### Other methane sources

**[44:12] Yishan:** Are there other known methane sources where you can apply essentially the same technology—capture gas from a fixed location and burn it?

**Olya:** Yes. The original permafrost sources were methane hydrates or natural-gas pockets, not manure. Abandoned coal mines are another example. Even after a mine closes, methane can continue moving through the ground because sealing every path to the surface is difficult. We’ve completed a project at a closed coal mine.

There are several other sources we’ve considered but haven’t yet addressed.

Large sources have a reasonable chance of becoming regulated. Smaller, distributed sources are less likely to be regulated because proving and enforcing every individual case is difficult. Those are the sources we concentrate on. Detection and enforcement can play a larger role when regulators can apply a big stick to a big source.

### Why not sell the methane as fuel?

**[46:03] Yishan:** Methane is also a fuel. Why not capture it and use it? Is transportation the problem?

**Olya:** Fuel-grade gas is around 99% methane. The gas from a manure pond is nowhere near that pure. You have to remove hydrogen sulfide almost completely or a pipeline won’t accept it. You also have to filter ammonia, CO₂, and other contaminants.

By the time you purify the gas and transport it to the nearest pipeline, the economics are poor. You can burn it in a generator, but then you need a generator able to tolerate variable gas composition and highly inconsistent flow.

**[47:06] Olya:** Productive use can make sense for a very large source because every project has fixed costs. For smaller sources, utilization may be around twenty times as expensive as simply destroying the methane.

Natural gas is very cheap in the United States after decades of technological improvement. A small biogas source cannot compete with fossil gas.

Large landfills and municipal wastewater plants can be big enough to justify purification and pipeline injection. They’re also generally regulated to at least burn their methane, so they aren’t Frost’s target.

### Possible uses for heat and CO₂

**[48:23] Olya:** For smaller systems, we’re exploring local uses for the waste heat, such as drying. Where livestock farms are near greenhouses, the greenhouse may also want the CO₂.

The challenge is that gas flow peaks in summer or early fall. Waste heat would be more valuable in winter, when you could use it to warm a barn. In a hot summer, nobody wants extra heat.

### Cow burps

**[49:32] Yishan:** Are there enormous methane sources that this combustion approach cannot solve?

**Olya:** Cow burps are a major one.

**[50:04] Olya:** Enteric methane from cattle is almost as large a source as oil and gas in terms of its atmospheric impact.

**Yishan:** I had heard about cow burps but didn’t know whether it was partly a joke. The scale is that large?

**Olya:** Yes. Frost’s equipment won’t solve that because the source is diffuse and moving. It requires a biological approach.

Possible solutions include feed supplements, vaccines, or changing the cattle gut microbiome so the bacteria produce less methane.

### The grass-fed paradox

**[50:51] Yishan:** In silvopasture, people sometimes argue that restoring pasture and changing a cow’s diet makes the digestion system more sustainable. Does grass feeding reduce methane?

**[51:34] Olya:** The data I’ve seen doesn’t fully support that. Grass-fed cows appear to burp methane at a rate similar to grain-fed cattle. Some seaweed supplements have shown reductions, but simply being grass-fed is different.

The unfortunate part is that grass-fed cattle take longer to reach maturity—perhaps 18 months instead of 12. If the methane rate is similar but the animal lives six months longer, its total climate impact is greater.

That creates a difficult ethical calculus. People may prefer a cow to have a more natural life, but the climate math can favor intensive production.
